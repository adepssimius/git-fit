### On the stride proposal — not before this race

**The mechanism he describes points at stride correctly.** Pressure into the nail bed from
beneath is plantar loading through the hallux at toe-off, and stride length, cadence and push-off
mechanics are what govern it. The reasoning is sound.

**The timing is wrong, and that is the whole objection.** Race day is 2026-10-17, six weeks out.
A deliberate large gait change is the highest-effort, highest-risk and least-certain intervention
available, and taper is the worst window in the block to attempt one:

- His limiters in this block have been **tissue, every time** (`log/2026-08-15.md`), with an
  active ITB entry in `athlete/profile.md` § Injuries.
- A large stride change redistributes load to structures that have not been trained for it —
  achilles, plantar fascia, calf. Those are precisely the tissues the walking-ramp cap exists to
  protect, and they announce themselves quietly a week or two before they stop a block.
- The payoff arrives long after October, and the risk arrives immediately.

**The small version already exists in this repo and is already proven for him.**
`athlete/profile.md` records, from 08-15: *"raise cadence 5-10%, walk every descent, stay off
camber"* — established mid-run management that worked. Raising cadence 5-10% shortens stride and
lowers peak per-step hallux load without rebuilding anything. **That is the version to run before
this race.** The full gait project is a good off-season question and a bad October one.

## THE LIGHTING FAILED — and this is the most important finding of the weekend

Athlete, 2026-09-07: *"My lighting strategy didn't work very well. I carried 2 headlamps plus a
very bright flashlight, and the flashlight ran out of batteries. That was my tool to see if there
was wildlife on the trail like the porcupine (I have had several night time porcupine encounters
on the race course). This was a little over one lap, so obviously that's not going to work. I
need a better light source. One of the headlamps died after about 80% of a lap."*

**Two of three light sources died inside 4h26 of darkness.** The flashlight — the wildlife
detection tool — ran flat. One headlamp died at ~80% of a lap.

### The race-night arithmetic

Race starts **Saturday 09:00**, cutoff Sunday 15:00 (`races/2026-10-17-ghost-train.md`).
Computed for the course at 42.80N, 71.66W:

| | |
|---|---|
| Sunset Sat 10-17 | **18:01** |
| Full dark from | **18:30** |
| First light Sun 10-18 | **06:35** |

**He needs roughly 2.7x the burn time he just failed to deliver.** 4h26 killed two of three

### What the fix actually is

*"I need a better light source"* is half of it. The other half is that this is a **power budget**,
not a brightness problem — and the crew geometry already solves it if it is planned:

- **Burn time at working brightness is the number that matters**, not rated lumens. Lamps
  advertise peak output for a few minutes, then step down; what matters is regulated runtime at
  the level he actually runs at, measured, not from the box.
- **Crew contact is every ~12km on race day**, and the plan already routes him past the
  start/finish and the 7.5mi turnaround. **That is a battery-swap cadence** — roughly every
  90-120 minutes at his pace. No lamp needs to survive 12 hours; it needs to survive one leg.
- **Carry spare cells, not spare lamps.** Two dead headlamps and a dead flashlight is three
  objects of dead weight; the same mass in cells or a battery pack is hours of light.
- **The wildlife light is a distinct role from the running light** — long throw, used
  intermittently. If it drained inside one lap it was either running continuously or starting
  well below full charge. Worth knowing which, because the fixes differ.
- **October is colder than September.** Lithium holds up better than alkaline in cold; a pack
  carried against the body holds up better than one exposed on a headband.
- `races/2026-10-17-ghost-train.md` already lists *"headlamp + spare batteries"* in the
  start/finish bag. **The bag is right and the plan around it is not** — spares at the
  start/finish only help if the swap is scheduled rather than reactive.

### The athlete's proposed fix, and the one thing wrong with it

Athlete, 2026-09-07: *"I think I need to get an 18650 headlamp and light, and carry 2 extra cells
with me that get swapped at either of the end aid stations when I have a failure."*

**The core of this is right and it is the important part.** Standardising both the headlamp and
the wildlife light on **18650** makes every spare fungible — one cell type, either light, no
sorting in the dark with cold hands. That single decision fixes most of what went wrong on 09-06,
where three devices meant three separate power stories and two of them ended.

**The flaw is "when I have a failure."** That is a reactive trigger, and a dead light is exactly
the event to design out rather than recover from — it happens mid-leg, in the dark, on a trail
with a known porcupine population, kilometres from the swap point. **Swap on schedule, not on
failure:** at a crew stop, before the cell is empty, on a cadence derived from measured burn time.
A planned swap costs thirty seconds standing still; an unplanned one costs whatever the trail
decides.

**Two spare cells is the right thing to carry and the wrong thing to own.** Rough arithmetic
against 12h05 of darkness, using placeholder runtimes that must be replaced by measurement:

| | |
|---|---|
| Darkness to cover | **12h05** (18:30 → 06:35) |
| Headlamp, continuous, ~4-6h per cell | **2-3 cells** |
| Wildlife light, intermittent | **1-2 cells** |
| Second headlamp, backup, mostly off | 1 cell |
| **Night total** | **~4-6 cells, plus margin** |

So: **2 on the body as immediate backup, and the night's full supply in the start/finish bag**,
restocked every time he passes. Carrying two and never restocking them is the same failure one
lap later. A cheap 18650 charger and a power bank at the start/finish turns four cells into
effectively unlimited, at the cost of one more thing for the crew to run.

**Check the swap geometry before committing to it.** `races/2026-10-17-ghost-train.md` gives crew
access at the start/finish **and** the 7.5mi turnaround — contact every ~12.1km, roughly every
100 minutes at his pace, so about seven swap opportunities across the night. **But that assumes
crew at both points.** If crew is only at the start/finish, opportunities drop to once per 24.1km
lap — roughly every 3h20, about 3-4 times all night — and the per-cell runtime needs to clear a
full lap with margin. **That is a crew-staffing question that changes the gear requirement**, and
it is not answered anywhere in the repo.

**Four practical notes:**

- **Measure before buying.** Rated lumens are a marketing number; lamps run peak output for a few
  minutes and then step down. The figure that matters is regulated runtime at the brightness he
  actually runs at. **09-11 is in four days and is the place to measure it.**
- **Cold costs capacity.** Overnight temperatures in the 40s°F will take something off every cell.
  Spares carried in a pocket against the body do better than spares in an outside pack pouch.
- **Buy cells from a reputable source.** Counterfeit and over-rated 18650s are endemic; a cell
  claiming 6000mAh is lying. Protected button-top cells are the safer default for headlamps.
- **Never be down to one light.** With two headlamps plus the flashlight, one death mid-leg still
  leaves two. The rule is: reach the next crew stop with at least one working light, then swap
  everything there — not just the dead one.

**This belongs in `races/2026-10-17-ghost-train.md` § crew/kit eventually**, alongside the
existing *"headlamp + spare batteries"* line, which is correct as far as it goes and has no
schedule attached to it. Flagged, not written — that edit is the athlete's call.

### The wildlife problem is specific, repeated, and this course's own

*"I have had several night time porcupine encounters on the race course."* Not a one-off: a known
recurring hazard on this specific trail after dark, which is why the flashlight had a job in the
first place. **Losing that light is the safety half of this finding**, separate from the
navigation half — and on 09-06 it is what forced the slow sections that contaminated the pace

### Hunger at hour 4 is the finding, and it is not an energy problem

`rules/fueling.md` § "The one remaining gap" frames the risk as *palatability collapsing at hour
12 to 20*, and the strategy as *"gels while they're palatable, then real food. The timer never
changes — only what's on the beep."*

**Hunger arriving at hour 4 is the leading indicator of that, showing up eight hours earlier than
the file anticipates trouble.** It is a different signal from aversion — he did not report gels
becoming unappealing, and energy was fine. Gels deliver carbohydrate without delivering satiety,
and the gap between those two opens long before the gels stop going down.

**That is useful rather than alarming, because it gives the switch point a first number.** The
file knows *what* to do (swap what is on the beep) but has never had an estimate of *when*. This
run says the earliest useful moment to have real food available at a crew stop is **around hour
4** — which on race day is roughly **13:00 Saturday**, well inside the daylight laps and long
before the night, when eating is easy and the crew is fresh.

### What he actually wanted tells us what to put in the crew bag

*"I was craving a grilled cheese or something salty with carbs."*

**Savoury, salty, warm, solid — the opposite of a gel on every axis.** This is the single most
useful sentence in the fueling record so far, because `rules/fueling.md` prescribes *"real food"*
for weeks 12-15 without ever saying what real food means for this athlete. Now it does.

**Read it as flavour fatigue, not sodium deficit.** `rules/fueling.md` records his sweat rate as
low-average and his electrolytes as *"dosed high and deliberately so,"* and he had taken an
electrolyte drink during this run. A salt craving on top of generous supplementation is far more
likely to be four hours of unrelieved sweet than a genuine sodium shortfall. That distinction
matters: the fix is **contrast**, not more salt.

**One practical caveat on the grilled cheese specifically.** Fat and protein slow gastric
emptying, and he is running a 90 g/hr carbohydrate rate that depends on the gut keeping up. The
craving is for *salty + carbs*; grilled cheese is the high-fat expression of that. The lower-fat
versions deliver the same relief at less gastric cost:

| what he craved | lower-fat equivalents that scratch the same itch |
|---|---|
| grilled cheese | salted boiled potatoes, pretzels, white rice, plain salted noodles |
| warm + savoury | **broth or instant ramen** — salt, warmth and fluid in one, and the classic overnight aid-station answer |
| salty + carbs | salted crackers, tortilla, pierogi |

Not a rule, and not an argument against the grilled cheese — if it goes down and stays down at
hour 4 in daylight, that is a good trade. It is an argument for **having both** staged, so the
fatty option is a choice rather than the only savoury thing in the cooler.

**Race-day logistics this implies, which nothing in the repo currently covers:**

- **Warm savoury food requires crew capability** — a stove, a thermos, or a cooler and a plan.
  That is a different ask than handing over gels, and it needs to be in the crew brief.
- Crew contact is every ~12km at the start/finish and the 7.5mi turnaround, so warm food is
  deliverable roughly every 90-120 minutes **if it is staged in advance.**
- `races/2026-10-17-ghost-train.md` notes **temperatures in the 40s°F** overnight. Warm savoury
  food at 02:00 is doing three jobs at once — calories, salt, and being warm — and the second
  night-time value is easy to underrate from a September evening.

### One confound, and it cuts the other way from the usual

**This was a night run, and hour 4 landed at 00:30.** Hunger at half past midnight is partly just
hunger at half past midnight — he had been awake all day and dinner was hours behind him. On race
day, hour 4 falls at 13:00, in the middle of the day, with breakfast at 07:00 and a normal eating
rhythm behind it. The circadian and meal-timing context is completely different.

**So hour 4 is a first estimate and probably an early one**, not a measured threshold. The right
reading is "real food needs to be available from the early crew stops onward," not "hunger begins
at 240 minutes." What would sharpen it is the same observation on a daytime long run.

**Electrolytes:** taken at a stop rather than carried, consistent with `rules/fueling.md`'s
*"dosed high and deliberately so ... keep doing it."* Sodium and fluid targets in that file are
still a `TODO` and this run does not close them — no volume or mg was recorded.
