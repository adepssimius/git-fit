## The nail finding is a race-day finding, not a shoe one

Pulled out on its own because it was nearly lost as a footnote to a question it does not answer.

**Onset is ~20km, in any shoe.** On race day he crosses 20km inside the first lap of seven
(`races/2026-10-17-ghost-train.md`: one lap = 24.1km). So:

- **Nail soreness is a certainty from lap 1**, not a risk to be managed away.
- At the A-goal of ~100km he spends roughly **80km above the onset threshold**; at the 100-mile
  stretch tier, ~140km of it.
- It is therefore **baseline discomfort to be planned around, not a warning sign.** That
  distinction matters at a crew stop, where `athlete/profile.md` makes "feet: hot spots vs actual
  damage" one of six go/no-go criteria. **Big-toe nail soreness is not a stop criterion for this
  athlete** — reacting to it as one would burn crew time and possibly a shoe change on a symptom
  that was always going to appear.

**What is not known, and matters more than anything else here:** whether it *plateaus* after 20km
or keeps escalating with distance. At 30km on 09-06 he called it *"a little toenail pain."* If it
stays little from 30km to 160km it is a non-issue; if it scales, it becomes a real limiter deep in
the race, and subungual bruising or nail loss is the failure mode to watch for.

**He has a data point nobody has asked him about: the 50k on this course ~3 years ago**
(`athlete/profile.md` § Context). What his big toe did over 50km, and whether he lost the nail,
is worth more on this question than anything the remaining seven weeks can generate.

### Current state, and what he proposes to do about it

Athlete, 2026-09-07: *"My nails are already messed up and bruised from previous runs in my Lone
Peaks. Losing toenails is a fact of life for me unless you have a magic remedy. I think the fix
is to modify my stride by a lot."*

**So he starts the race with nails already bruised.** That is a materially different position from
starting with healthy nails, and it was not previously recorded anywhere.

**Separate the two failure modes — they have completely different race consequences.**

| | gradual bruising / eventual nail loss | an acute nail event mid-race |
|---|---|---|
| what it is | subungual bleeding accumulating over hours | an already-lifting nail catching and tearing |
| when | every long run he has ever done | one step, unpredictable |
| race impact | discomfort; **not a limiter** | sharp pain, possible stop, infection risk |
| preventable? | not really, for him | **yes, cheaply** |

The block has been treating these as one thing. **Only the second one can end a race**, and it is
the one that responds to preparation. The first is a cosmetic and comfort cost he has already
priced in.

### On the stride proposal — not before this race

**The mechanism he describes points at stride correctly.** Pressure into the nail bed from
beneath is plantar loading through the hallux at toe-off, and stride length, cadence and push-off
mechanics are what govern it. The reasoning is sound.

**The timing is wrong, and that is the whole objection.** Race day is 2026-10-17, six weeks out.
A deliberate large gait change is the highest-effort, highest-risk and least-certain intervention
available, and taper is the worst window in the block to attempt one:

- His limiters in this block have been **tissue, every time** (`log/2026-08-15.md`), with an
  active ITB entry in `athlete/profile.md` § Injuries.
- A large stride change redistributes load to structures that have not been trained for it —
  achilles, plantar fascia, calf. Those are precisely the tissues the walking-ramp cap exists to
  protect, and they announce themselves quietly a week or two before they stop a block.
- The payoff arrives long after October, and the risk arrives immediately.

**The small version already exists in this repo and is already proven for him.**
`athlete/profile.md` records, from 08-15: *"raise cadence 5-10%, walk every descent, stay off
camber"* — established mid-run management that worked. Raising cadence 5-10% shortens stride and
lowers peak per-step hallux load without rebuilding anything. **That is the version to run before
this race.** The full gait project is a good off-season question and a bad October one.

### One thing nobody has checked: every shoe he owns is an Altra

`athlete/profile.md` § Footwear lists five pairs — Lone Peak 9, two Mont Blanc Carbons,
Experience Flow 2, Escalante. **All the same brand, and Altra's trail line is zero-drop.**

If the mechanism really is plantar pressure under the big toe, **heel-to-toe drop is a direct
lever on it**, and zero drop shifts load forward. "It happens in every shoe" carries much less
information than it appears to when every shoe is drawn from one unusually low-drop brand — a
variable held constant across the whole sample looks exactly like a variable that does not
matter. **This is the same reasoning error this entry already made once today**, and it is worth
naming before it is made a third time.

Two cheap checks, neither requiring a gait overhaul:

1. **Has he ever run more than 20km in the road shoes?** The 09-05 Experience Flow run was
   10.84km — *below his own 20km onset threshold*. If every 20km+ run in his history has been in
   a zero-drop trail shoe, then "shoe-independent" is really "trail-shoe-independent, road
   untested," and the conclusion is weaker than it reads.
2. **What is the actual drop on the Experience Flow 2?** Altra's Experience line is the company's
   non-zero-drop range, so this pair may already differ from the rest — worth confirming from the
   spec rather than assumed either way. If it does differ, he may already own the comparison.

## No ITB, at 30km

The ITB entered `athlete/profile.md` § Injuries on 08-15, when it arrived at ~20km — *5km earlier
than its usual onset*, and the entry's hypothesis was that 14 minutes at or above LTHR banked in
the first 72 minutes brought it forward.

**30.08km at 8:37/km, and nothing.** That is 10km past the 08-15 onset point and 5km past its
historical one, at a genuinely easy intensity.

**Two explanations, and they are not rivals.** The 08-15 hypothesis was intensity: take the
threshold work out and the ITB stays away. That holds. But he also **walked every steep descent**,
and `athlete/profile.md` already records descent-walking as established ITB management from that
same 08-15 session — *"raise cadence 5-10%, walk every descent, stay off camber."*

So this is the ITB protocol being followed, and working, over a longer distance than it has been
tested at before. That is a better result than "the ITB went away," because it is repeatable: it
names the behaviour to keep on race day rather than crediting luck. It is the first ITB-free long
run since the injury was recorded.

## Correction to this entry's first draft

Written 2026-09-07 before he was asked, this entry called the weekend a deliberate restructure of
the Big Day and argued the executed shape served the plan's intent better than the plan's own
shape would have.

**There was no restructure.** He was not working from the plan at all — he had a general sense
that big miles were due and went to run a lap. The resemblance to the Big Day weekend is
coincidental.

What survives that correction, and what does not:

- **Survives:** the training stimulus. Four hours running into and through midnight is night
  rehearsal whether or not it was labelled as such, and the body does not care about intent.
- **Does not survive:** any claim that the weekend *rehearsed* the race's night phase. Rehearsal
  implies the kit, the fueling and the crew routine were being tested against a plan. They were
  not being tested against anything.
- **Strengthened, if anything:** the fitness read. He did this spontaneously, on a head cold,
  three weeks after a fever, and reported it as a 4.
