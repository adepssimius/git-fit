# ⚠ THE REAL FINDING: GASTRIC SHUTDOWN AT ~32 KM AND A NEAR-SYNCOPE AT HOME

**Everything above is secondary to this.** Reported by the athlete after the bath, 2026-09-13.

## What happened

**On the run, from roughly 32 km (~4h20) — the front half of lap 2:**

- *"I was having trouble hydrating... My stomach was not emptying so I could not get more fluid in."*
- **Urine output collapsed.** Every ~30 minutes early in the session; **four stops across the whole
  of lap 2, with little output at each.**

**At home, after the bath:**

- Two bites of dry chicken, scratchy going down, **nausea**. Could not eat more.
- Walking upstairs: **intense nausea, cold sweats, light-headed.** Took a bowl in case he vomited.
- At the end of the hallway, **felt he was about to pass out.** Lay flat on the floor and called his
  wife.
- **BP 88/55**, against a normal of ~112/72. She (an RN, present) read it as a **vaso-vagal event**.
- **Recovered to 105/65 over the following hour** with ~1400 ml of oral rehydration solution, on top
  of ~500 ml of plain water on the drive home.

## The reading, and the first thing it does is overturn a finding written above

**~~Gel palate fatigue at 4h37.~~ — RE-READ 2026-09-13, and this is a correction, not an addition.**

The entry above treats *"I am getting sick of gels"* at 33.63 km / 4h37 as **taste aversion**.
**It almost certainly was not.** It sits within a kilometre or two of the point he now reports his
stomach stopped emptying, and the two are the same event seen from different angles: **a gut that
has stopped clearing does not want another gel.** The aversion was a symptom, not a preference.

**This matters because the two readings imply opposite responses.** Palate fatigue says *vary the
flavours, bring real food*. Gastric stasis says *reduce the concentration and the rate*. The
entry above recommended leaning harder on the flask. **That was the wrong instruction and it may
have made the back half worse.**

## The leading hypothesis, and it is testable

**The drink was very concentrated. Tailwind High Carb at 90 g per 700 ml is ~129 g/L.** Sports
drinks formulated to empty quickly sit nearer 60-80 g/L. **He was running ~90 g/hr of gel on top of
a ~13% solution**, and gastric emptying slows as carbohydrate concentration rises.

**This is a change of variable that is unique to this session.** Every previous long run was
gels-plus-water; `log/2026-09-06.md` held ~90 g/hr for 4h19 with *"no GI distress"* and
`log/2026-09-07.md` reached ~118 g/hr clean. **The one thing that was different last night is the
carrier**, and the entry above praised that change as a race-day upgrade. It needs testing before
it is trusted again.

**Three candidate contributors, and they are not mutually exclusive:**

1. **Carbohydrate concentration** — the hypothesis above. **Dilute the Tailwind and retest.**
2. **Duration.** 7h14 against a previous maximum of 4h19. Gastric emptying degrades with time
   under load and he had never asked it for this long.
3. **A developing fluid deficit feeding itself.** Reduced emptying means less fluid absorbed, which
   reduces plasma volume, which further reduces splanchnic blood flow and emptying. Once started it
   compounds, which fits the reported timeline exactly.

**Sodium is unlikely to be the deficit** — he was on a sodium-rich mix and ate ~15 olives. The
picture of falling urine output plus orthostatic hypotension points at **volume**, not sodium.

## The collapse itself — his wife was right, and there is an antecedent worth naming

**Vaso-vagal is a fair description of the event and she was standing there.** What the log should
also record is the state he arrived in: **he had been unable to take fluid on for the last ~18 km,
his urine output had collapsed, and he then sat in a hot bath.**

**Hot water vasodilates.** Doing that on a reduced plasma volume, after seven hours of running, and
